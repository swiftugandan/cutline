Design the beam. Check it against R149.

Cutline traces light through an LED projector or a reflector, ray by ray. It aims the beam the way an approval laboratory does and measures every point, line and zone of UN Regulation No. 149. It also checks any lamp's light distribution against every market, and where lamps sit on the vehicle. It runs in your browser, and your files stay on your computer.

Aimed the way the laboratory aims it

R149 measures a lamp after it has been aimed by its own cut-off. Cutline follows the instrumental method of Annex 6, so a beam that looks right on screen also has to sit right after aiming.

  1. Find the cut-off

    A scan rises through the beam at 2.5° on the driver's side, in steps of 0.05°. The steepest fall of log intensity marks the cut-off, and it goes onto line B, 0.57° below the horizon.

  2. Aim it sideways

    By the method the applicant chooses: three vertical scans across the rising edge, or one scan along the line 0.2° down. Either way, the elbow lands where the regulation puts it.

  3. Measure through the receiver

    Every value is the average over a 65 mm receiver at 25 m. Where too few rays arrive, the receiver widens and the value carries its error, so noise is never mistaken for a pass.

Any lamp, every market

Open an IES or EULUMDAT file of a headlamp or a signal lamp, or send the beam you traced. Cutline checks it against each market by that market's own laboratory method, and against your own targets.

  1. See it as an engineer does

    Isolux at 25 m or intensity, on the road surface or a target, in false colour or heat, on a scale you set. A uniformity map shows each direction against its surroundings, so dark patches and stripes stand out and the cut-off does not.

  2. Check every market

    UN R149 for right- and left-hand traffic, UN R123, UN R148 for signal lamps, FMVSS 108, CMVSS 108, Taiwan's VSTD and India's AIS drafts. Each value cites its source; national rules show which UN series they stand on.

  3. Share the report

    One HTML file with the summary, the pictures, every requirement and your targets, ready to print to PDF. Markets Cutline cannot check from an official source are listed with the reason, never guessed.

Where the lamps sit on the vehicle

Open an STL, OBJ or glTF model of the vehicle and click where each lamp goes. Cutline checks UN R48 and FMVSS 108: presence and number for the vehicle's category, heights to the apparent surface, distance from the outer edge, the separation of each pair, and the angles of geometric visibility, casting rays to find any bodywork that hides a lamp.

What it does not do

Cutline is for concept design. A passing result means the simulated design meets the limits, not that a lamp would pass at a technical service.

  • Type-approval photometry only. No colour, run-up time, conformity-of-production allowances, bend lighting or adaptive driving beams.
  • One optical unit, one LED. A production headlamp often combines several modules. The reflector's starting design still fails a few Class C requirements.
  • Simple scattering. Lens texture and outer-lens haze are modelled; dust, ageing and heat are not.
  • One distribution at a time. Rules comparing two states of a lamp, such as stop to tail ratios, are listed for you to check.
  • Meshes, not STEP. Export the vehicle from CAD as STL, OBJ or glTF. Rules relative to the rear window or other lamps are listed to check by hand.
  • Sources you can check. Every limit cites the official text, and the documentation lists the choices each regulation leaves open.
